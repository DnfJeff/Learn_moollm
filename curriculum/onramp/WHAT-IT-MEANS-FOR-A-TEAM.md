# What It Means for a Team

> **Tier:** Onramp · deeper: [kit/RETROFIT.md](../kit/RETROFIT.md) · the ideas behind this: [THE-TEN-IDEAS.md](THE-TEN-IDEAS.md)

One page, for the person deciding whether this is worth anyone's time.

**The premise in one sentence:** everything your AI assistant knows — its instructions, its
know-how, what it's working on, why it did what it did — becomes ordinary written documents
that live with the project and that anyone on the team can open, read, and fix.

## The four returns

**Cost.** Assistants bill by how much they read and write. An assistant with a table of
contents reads the two pages it needs instead of the whole shelf; the measured version of
this claim (a working session touching about 4% of a very large library, chosen from a
catalog 155 times smaller than the library) is on the technical shelf, in
[the evidence document](../spec/EVIDENCE.md). The everyday version: less re-reading, less
re-explaining, smaller bills.

**Speed.** Sessions start already oriented — the briefing sheet and the project binder
replace the daily warm-up ritual. And work that used to take a committee of separate AI
calls (each one slow, each one re-fed the whole story) can often happen in one sitting.

**Continuity.** The work survives. A session ending, a laptop dying, a person leaving,
a different AI tool being adopted next quarter — the binder, the rules, and the library
are files in the project, so none of those events resets the assistant to zero. You are
also, deliberately, **not locked in**: plain documents move anywhere.

**Trust.** This is the quiet one that ends up mattering most. When the assistant misbehaves,
there is something to *look at* — the rule it should have followed, the note saying what it
thought the task was, the log of what it decided. Reviews become possible. Audits become
possible. "Why did it do that?" stops being a mystery and becomes a page with a wrong line
on it, and wrong lines can be fixed.

## What it costs

Honesty section — there are three real costs:

1. **An editing habit.** The catalog entries and summaries are written by people, and kept
   good by people. This is ongoing work — modest, but real, like keeping any good runbook.
2. **A tidiness habit.** The "what I'm working on" note only helps if it's current. Stale
   notes are worse than none.
3. **One small automatic check**, run routinely, that verifies the team's own rule sheets
   aren't quietly broken — because a helpful assistant absorbs your paperwork errors so
   gracefully that no human ever notices them.

## What it is not

- **Not a product.** Nothing to buy, no vendor. It's a way of organizing documents,
  published openly. (It comes from a system called MOOLLM, by Don Hopkins.)
- **Not a guarantee machine.** Posted rules make bad behavior rare, not impossible. Rules
  that must *never* break — spending limits, security steps — still belong in hard
  controls, and the approach says so itself rather than overselling.
- **Not all-or-nothing.** Adoption is a staircase with a landing at every step. Step one is
  a one-afternoon change (give the assistant a binder) that pays for itself in the first
  week. Teams stop at whatever step matches their size; most small teams should stop early,
  and that's the design, not a failure to finish.

## The decision, simply

If your team uses AI assistance more than casually, the first two steps — the binder and
the posted rules — are cheap enough that the question isn't really *whether* but *who
spends the afternoon*. The later steps (the library, the catalog, the adapters) earn their
keep only at scale, and the staircase is built so you can't over-invest by accident if you
climb it in order.

the same staircase, with instructions.
